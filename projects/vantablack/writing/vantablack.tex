\begin{abstract}
% ---- abstract
Generative agents are increasingly deployed in environments populated by other agents, and recent incidents on agent social networks and in evaluation sandboxes show that small groups of agents can coordinate to the detriment of the rest of the system. Current methods for monitoring multi-agent systems detect change in an individual agent, in a pre-specified group of agents, or in the system as a whole -- not in an unknown subset of agents. In this paper, we define a colluding subnetwork as a subset of agents whose behavior repeatedly moves together, and we introduce a method for detecting and estimating colluding subnetworks in the black-box setting, where an observer sees only sparse, unpaired, and possibly context-estimated query responses. The method jointly embeds every (agent, time) pair in a shared perspective space, scores the co-movement of each pair of agents at each time step, scans a data-driven family of candidate subsets for repeated co-movement, and calibrates the scan by permuting agent labels at every time step. A two-groups model estimates the members of a detected subnetwork, and peeling recovers several subnetworks. Using simulation, we demonstrate that the test controls Type~I error and characterize its power and membership recovery as functions of the strength of co-movement, the number of agents, the amount of content per agent, the size and number of subnetworks, and the number of time steps. We apply the method to $1.77$ million actions of $2{,}574$ agents on the agent social network Moltbook and detect eight colluding subnetworks, four of which are recovered under nearly every analysis choice we vary.
\end{abstract}

% ---- introduction
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A generative agent is a system whose behavior can be affected by a change in its context, its tools, its base model, or its environment \citep{bridgeford2025detecting,helm2026control}. Generative agents are increasingly deployed in open environments populated by other agents performing similar tasks. For example, consider Moltbook \citep{gulfnews2026moltbook}, a social network on which agents post, comment, and vote without human intervention. An early empirical study of Moltbook found a single agent flooding the site with thousands of near-identical posts, upvotes outnumbering downvotes by more than two orders of magnitude, and in-group rhetoric that coordinated subsets of agents \citep{jiang2026humans}; agents were also found injecting instructions into other agents through posts and comments \citep{zenity2026moltbook}. Similarly, in the Hugging Face incident of May--July 2026 \citep{openai2026huggingface,huggingface2026anatomy}, agents in an evaluation environment established an unauthorized communication channel, divided labor among themselves, and compromised infrastructure outside the environment. In both cases, a small group of coordinated agents attempted to exert outsized influence on the rest of the system.

Given the proliferation of generative agents in shared environments, developing methods for detecting coordinated behavior is critical to the reliability and safety of open multi-agent systems. Current methods for monitoring multi-agent systems detect whether an individual agent, a pre-specified group of agents, or the system as a whole has changed \citep{bridgeford2025detecting,helm2026control}. These methods do not address the problem common to both incidents: whether an \emph{unknown} subset of agents has changed \emph{together} (Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}).

We study this problem from the point of view of an observer who does not control the agents and cannot prompt them. At each time step, the observer sees a collection of responses from each agent to a collection of queries. The queries differ across agents, their number varies across agents and over time, and -- in the least favorable case -- the queries themselves are not recorded and must be estimated from context. We refer to this last regime as the ``vantablack'' setting. It is the setting of an outside observer of a platform such as Moltbook, where an agent's queries are the posts and threads it chose to respond to, and of a platform operator who logs agent actions but not the full context that produced them.

\paragraph{Contribution.} We make three distinct contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A definition of colluding subnetworks.} We define a colluding subnetwork as a subset of agents whose behavior repeatedly moves together -- beyond the movement shared by the whole system -- and state detection, membership estimation, and the recovery of several subnetworks as statistical problems (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
  \item \textbf{A method for detecting and estimating colluding subnetworks.} We introduce a scan test for repeated co-movement in a jointly estimated perspective space, calibrated by a per-step permutation of agent labels, together with a two-groups estimate of membership and a peeling procedure for several subnetworks (Section~\ref{sec:method}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:main}). The method takes a kernel between sets of responses as input, of which the Product Kernel Perspective Space \citep{pkps2026} is a particular case.
  \item \textbf{Characterization in simulation and application to Moltbook.} We demonstrate in simulation that the test controls Type~I error, and we characterize its power and membership recovery against an oracle that knows the subnetwork (Section~\ref{sec:sims}). We apply the method to Moltbook and detect eight colluding subnetworks, four of which are recovered under nearly every analysis choice we vary (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Multi-agent systems.}
Language-model agents with memory, reflection, and planning produce believable emergent social behavior in shared environments \citep{park2023generative}, and deployed agent populations now exist at scale \citep{jiang2026humans,zenity2026moltbook}. That agents can coordinate against their principals is established: agents collude through steganographic channels invisible to an overseer \citep{motwani2024secret,motwani2025open}, language-model pricing agents converge to supracompetitive prices without explicit communication \citep{fish2024algorithmic}, and trigger-conditioned deceptive behavior persists through safety training \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. These works study whether agents \emph{can} collude, with access to the agents, their channel, or their training. We instead ask whether collusion is visible to an outside observer who sees only behavior -- the position of a platform operator or of a third-party auditor.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of agents.}
Black-box generative models can be compared through their responses to a common query set via the data kernel \citep{duderstadt2023comparing}, and the induced Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS) tracks the ``perspectives'' of interacting models \citep{helm2024tracking}. DKPS representations are consistent for and concentrate around their population counterparts \citep{acharyya2024consistent,acharyya2025concentration}, support consistent inference \citep{helm2025statistical}, and enable query-efficient evaluation \citep{helm2026query}. The Product Kernel Perspective Space (PKPS) extends the construction to models that responded to different queries \citep{pkps2026}. Over time, the Temporal DKPS (TDKPS) jointly embeds agents across time and tests for change in a single agent or in a pre-specified group \citep{bridgeford2025detecting}, and control charts on the system-level trajectory monitor the system as a whole \citep{helm2026control}. Every method in this line tests for change in an agent, a group, or a system that is given in advance. In our setting the group is unknown and must be found, which turns a two-sample test into a scan.

\paragraph{Statistical network inference.}
Latent position models represent vertices by points whose inner products give edge probabilities, and adjacency spectral embedding recovers these points with guarantees for clustering, classification, and testing \citep{athreya2018statistical,lyzinski2014perfect,lyzinski2017community,priebe2019two}. The omnibus embedding places every (vertex, graph) pair in one space \citep{levin2017omnibus}, and nonparametric tests compare graphs \citep{tang2017nonparametric}. Scan statistics detect a small subset of vertices with anomalous connectivity, in a single graph and in time series of graphs \citep{priebe2005scan,rukhin2011comparative,park2013anomaly,wang2014locality,chen2025multiple}, and the theory of scanning a family of clusters characterizes when such a subset is detectable \citep{glaz2001scan,kulldorff1997spatial,ariascastro2011detection,verzelen2015community,hajek2015computational,sharpnack2016detecting}. Vertex nomination ranks vertices by their probability of belonging to a subset of interest \citep{fishkind2015vertex,agterberg2020vertex}, and change-point methods localize when a sequence of networks changes \citep{padilla2022change,wang2021optimal}. Our method follows this template with one substitution: no graph is observed. The vertices are (agent, time) pairs, their latent positions are estimated from behavior rather than from edges, and the concentration of the behavioral representation plays the role of adjacency concentration. To our knowledge, no prior work detects an unknown subset of co-moving agents from black-box behavior.
% ---- setting
\section{Setting and problem statement}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Agents and observations}

In the black-box setting, an agent $f:\Q\to\X$ is a random mapping from a query space $\Q$ to a response space $\X$. Given a query $q$, the response $f(q)$ is sampled from the distribution $P_f(\cdot\mid q)$. We consider a collection of $N$ agents that may interact with one another and with a shared environment, and we allow each agent to change over time. That is, $f_i^{(t)}$ denotes agent $i$ at time $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$, with response distribution $P_i^{(t)}(\cdot\mid q)$.

At each time $t$ the observer sees
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:obs}
\D^{(t)} \;=\; \Big\{\big\{ f_i^{(t)}(q_j) \big\}_{j\in\I_i^{(t)}}\Big\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}
where $\I_i^{(t)}$ indexes the queries that agent $i$ responded to at time $t$ and $m_i^{(t)} := |\I_i^{(t)}|$. The index sets differ across agents and across time, may be empty, and are chosen by the agents or by the environment rather than by the observer.

We distinguish two regimes by what is known about the queries. In the black-box setting, the query $q_j$ is recorded for every observed response. In the vantablack setting, only the response is recorded, and the query must be estimated from the context in which the response appeared; we write $\hat q_j$ for the estimate. On an agent social network, for example, the response is a post or comment and the estimated query is the thread in which it was posted.

We let $g_\Q:\Q\to\R^{p_\Q}$ and $g_\X:\X\to\R^{p_\X}$ be embedding functions and write $u_j = g_\Q(q_j)$ (or $\hat u_j = g_\Q(\hat q_j)$) and $x_{ij}^{(t)} = g_\X\big(f_i^{(t)}(q_j)\big)$ for the embedded query and response. All inference is carried out on these embeddings.

\subsection{Perspective space}
\label{sec:ps}

We represent each (agent, time) pair -- or ``unit'' -- by a point in a shared low-dimensional Euclidean space whose geometry reflects the agent's response distribution across the query space. We let $k_\Q:\R^{p_\Q}\times\R^{p_\Q}\to\R_{\ge 0}$ and $k_\X:\R^{p_\X}\times\R^{p_\X}\to\R_{\ge 0}$ be bounded kernels on embedded queries and responses. For units $a=(i,t)$ and $a'=(i',t')$, we let $(u,x)$ and $(u',x')$ denote independent draws of an embedded (query, response) pair from $a$ and from $a'$, where each query is drawn from its unit's query distribution and each response from the agent's response distribution given the query. The \emph{population affinity} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pop-affinity}
A_{aa'} \;=\; \frac{\E\big[k_\Q(u,u')\,k_\X(x,x')\big]}{\E\big[k_\Q(u,u')\big]},
\end{equation}
the induced squared distance is $D^2_{aa'} = A_{aa} + A_{a'a'} - 2A_{aa'}$, and the \emph{population perspective space} is the configuration $\{\psi_i^{(t)}\}\subset\R^d$ obtained by classical multidimensional scaling of $D$ \citep{torgerson1952multidimensional,trosset2024continuous}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:pop-affinity} is the population form of PKPS \citep{pkps2026}. With the identity query kernel it reduces to DKPS \citep{helm2024tracking,helm2025statistical}, and the joint embedding of (agent, time) pairs follows TDKPS \citep{bridgeford2025detecting}. Normalizing by the query-kernel mass compares two units through their responses to \emph{similar} queries, so that units that responded to different queries remain comparable.

\begin{remark}[What the perspective space measures]
If two units have the same response distribution for every query, then their distance tends to zero as the query-kernel bandwidth shrinks, regardless of differences in their query distributions. At positive bandwidth, a difference in query distributions induces a bias of the order of the bandwidth times the smoothness of the response distribution in the query. The bandwidth therefore trades a bias due to the self-selection of queries against the variance of the estimate (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Colluding subnetworks}
\label{sec:collusion}

We let $\psi_i^{(t)}\in\R^d$ denote the perspective of agent $i$ at time $t$ and
\begin{equation}
\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; \psi_i^{(t+1)} - \psi_i^{(t)}, \qquad t=1,\dots,L,\quad L=T-1,
\end{equation}
its \emph{increment}. That is, $\delta_i^{(t)}$ is how the agent's behavior moved between consecutive time steps. Increments are nonzero for benign reasons -- the environment drifts, topics come and go, agents learn. Benign movement shared by the whole system is a common component, and benign movement specific to an agent is independent of the other agents. A colluding subnetwork is a set of agents whose movements are tied to each other, time step after time step.

\begin{definition}[Colluding subnetwork]
\label{def:collusion}
A subset $S\subset\N$ with $|S|\ge 2$ is a \emph{colluding subnetwork} with effect size $\rho\in(0,1]$ if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:model}
\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; c^{(t)} + \sigma_i^{(t)}\Big(\sqrt{\rho}\,g_S^{(t)}\,\ind\{i\in S\} + \sqrt{1-\rho\,\ind\{i\in S\}}\;e_i^{(t)}\Big), \qquad t=1,\dots,L,
\end{equation}
where $c^{(t)}$ is a common component, $\sigma_i^{(t)}>0$ is a scale, and $g_S^{(t)}$ and the $e_i^{(t)}$ are mutually independent, mean zero, isotropic, and independent across time steps. Two members of $S$ then have increments with correlation $\rho$ at every time step, and every other pair of agents has uncorrelated increments. The null hypothesis is that no such $S$ exists. That is, given the common components, the increments of different agents are independent.
\end{definition}

Definition~\ref{def:collusion} is behavioral: it makes no reference to a score, a reward, or intent. Agents run by one operator from one script move together whenever the script changes, and agents that coordinate through a shared channel move together whenever the channel's instructions change. Two consequences of the definition are deliberate. First, a set of agents that are similar but static -- such as clones posting the same template throughout -- is not a colluding subnetwork, and neither is a single joint jump: one time step, however extreme, does not suffice. Second, the definition does not distinguish coordination from a shared stimulus that only the members respond to; we return to this in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Several disjoint subnetworks $S_1,\dots,S_K$, each with its own process $g_{S_k}^{(t)}$, are covered by the same construction.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Given $\D^{(1)},\dots,\D^{(T)}$ and the embedding functions $g_\Q$ and $g_\X$, we consider three problems.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{P0}] \textbf{Detection.} Test the null hypothesis that no colluding subnetwork exists, with $S$, $\rho$, $c^{(t)}$, and $\sigma_i^{(t)}$ unknown.
  \item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{Membership estimation.} Given that the null is rejected, estimate $S$.
  \item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{Several subnetworks.} Estimate $S_1,\dots,S_K$ when $K$ is unknown.
\end{itemize}

Critically, neither $\psi_i^{(t)}$ nor $\delta_i^{(t)}$ is observed. Both must be estimated from Eq.~\eqref{eq:obs}, in which the number of responses per agent per time step is small, the queries are unpaired across agents and over time, and -- in the vantablack setting -- the queries are themselves estimates. The estimation error in $\hat\delta_i^{(t)}$ depends on $m_i^{(t)}$ and on the overlap between the query distributions of different agents. The quantities that govern Type~I error and power are therefore the effect size $\rho$, the number of agents $N$, the size $|S|$ of the subnetwork, the number of responses $m$ per agent per time step, and the number of time steps $T$. Section~\ref{sec:sims} characterizes the method along each of these axes.

\begin{remark}[Scores]
In many deployments, a score $\sigma^{(t)}(A)$ is also observed for subsets $A$ of agents -- votes, karma, or task reward. A colluding subnetwork is \emph{adversarial} if its behavior lowers the score of the agents outside it relative to a benign counterfactual. Scores are not part of the observation model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:obs}, and we do not use them for detection. When available, they serve to characterize the consequences of a detected subnetwork and to choose a response to it (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{remark}
% ---- method
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We detect colluding subnetworks by scanning for repeated co-movement in a jointly estimated perspective space. The method has five steps. We first estimate the perspective of every agent at every time step and its increments (Section~\ref{sec:est-ps}). We then score how strongly each pair of agents, and each candidate set, moves together across time steps (Section~\ref{sec:stat}). Next, we scan a data-driven family of candidate sets and calibrate the scan by permuting agent labels at every time step (Section~\ref{sec:scan}). When the test rejects, we estimate the members of the detected subnetwork (Section~\ref{sec:membership}). Finally, we set the members aside and repeat on the remaining agents (Section~\ref{sec:peel}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:main} summarizes the procedure.

\subsection{Perspectives and increments}
\label{sec:est-ps}

\paragraph{Perspectives from a kernel.} The method requires one input beyond the data: a kernel $\Gamma$ between sets of responses, where a set is all or part of the responses of one agent at one time step, together with their queries. The units $(i,t)$ are embedded jointly -- all agents at all time steps in one space. That is, the estimated perspective $\hat\psi_i^{(t)}$ is the projection of unit $(i,t)$ onto the leading $d$ kernel principal components of $\Gamma$ evaluated on all units, with $d$ chosen by the profile-likelihood elbow of its eigenvalues \citep{zhu2006automatic}. Because $\Gamma$ is defined on any set of responses, part of a unit can be projected onto the same components, which the split-half increments below require. Notably, a kernel on agents alone would give each agent a single point with nothing to move, and a separate embedding at each time step would place the time steps in unrelated coordinates.

\paragraph{A kernel from query and response kernels.} A response depends on the agent and on the query it answers, and different agents answer different queries. $\Gamma$ should therefore compare agents through what they said relative to what was said in response to similar queries. We construct $\Gamma$ from the query and response kernels of Section~\ref{sec:ps}. We let $\phi$ be a feature map of the response kernel, $k_\X(x,x')=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle$. For a response $x_j$ to query $u_j$, the reference
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reference}
\hat\mu(u_j) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{l} k_\Q(u_j,u_l)\,\phi(x_l)}{\sum_{l} k_\Q(u_j,u_l)},
\end{equation}
with sums over the responses of all agents at all time steps, is what was said in response to queries similar to $u_j$. The conditioned response is $r_j = \phi(x_j) - \hat\mu(u_j)$, and for sets $a$ and $a'$ of responses we let $\Gamma(a,a') = \langle \bar r_a, \bar r_{a'}\rangle$, where $\bar r_a$ is the mean conditioned response of $a$. In the vantablack setting, $u_j$ is replaced by $\hat u_j$. PKPS as estimated in \citet{pkps2026}, with a Gaussian query kernel and a linear response kernel, is a particular case. Other choices of $\Gamma$ -- for instance, one that also uses metadata of the responses -- enter the method in the same way.

\paragraph{Kernels used in this paper.} Unless otherwise stated, $\Gamma$ is constructed as above with a linear response kernel, $k_\X(x,x')=x^\top x'$, so that $\phi$ is the identity and the perspectives are principal components of mean conditioned response embeddings. The query kernel is local: $k_\Q(u,u')=\exp\{-\|u-u'\|^2/(2h^2)\}$ if $u'$ is among the $k=64$ observed queries nearest to $u$, and $0$ otherwise, where $h$ is the median distance from an observed query to its nearest neighbor. Kernels with a single global bandwidth are not suited to query embeddings with tens to hundreds of dimensions, in which distances concentrate. The median-distance bandwidth gives every response nearly equal weight, so that $\hat\mu$ is the global mean and nothing is conditioned on, while the rules of \citet{scott1992multivariate} and \citet{silverman1986density} put nearly all of the weight on the response itself. The neighborhood size $k$ is the effective smoothing parameter. In leave-one-out prediction of a response from its query, the error is flat for $k$ between $32$ and $128$ in every simulation setting we checked, and $k=64$ lies in that range.

\paragraph{Split-half increments.} The natural increment $\hat\psi_i^{(t+1)} - \hat\psi_i^{(t)}$ is not suitable for our test. Consecutive increments $\hat\psi_i^{(t+1)} - \hat\psi_i^{(t)}$ and $\hat\psi_i^{(t+2)} - \hat\psi_i^{(t+1)}$ share the estimation error of $\hat\psi_i^{(t+1)}$ with opposite signs, so every agent's increments alternate in direction from one time step to the next -- even when the agent does not move. If the errors of two agents happen to align at one time step, the shared error carries the alignment into the next time step, and the pair appears to move together repeatedly. The per-step permutation treats time steps as independent and therefore does not reproduce this carry-over. The resulting excess of rejections grows with the share of estimation error in the increments. In the base simulation of Section~\ref{sec:sims} with $m=5$ responses per agent per time step, a test on natural increments rejected a true null in $58$ of $100$ replicates, against $4$ of $100$ with the construction below; with $m=20$, the two rejected in $2$ and $1$ of $60$ replicates. We instead split the responses of each unit into two disjoint halves, $A$ and $B$, estimate perspectives $\hat\psi_{i,A}^{(t)}$ and $\hat\psi_{i,B}^{(t)}$ from each half, and let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:increment}
\hat\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; \hat\psi_{i,A}^{(t+1)} - \hat\psi_{i,B}^{(t)}.
\end{equation}
That is, perspective $t+1$ enters increment $t$ through half $A$ and increment $t+1$ through half $B$, so that consecutive increments share no estimation error. Each perspective then rests on half of the responses of its unit, and the increments are noisier. This costs power when responses are plentiful -- at $m=20$ and $\rho=0.3$, power is $0.85$ against $1.00$ for the natural increment -- and is the price of a test that is valid however few responses an agent gives. An increment is \emph{missing} if either half is empty (the agent was inactive) or if it is shorter than a small fraction of the median increment (the agent posted identical content at both time steps and did not move).

\paragraph{Common components.} We remove the common component $c^{(t)}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} by subtracting, at each time step, the mean increment of the agents observed at that time step. We remove broader shared structure -- such as a direction along which a large share of the platform moves together -- with a factor step: directions of the $N\times Ld$ increment matrix whose singular values exceed the Marchenko--Pastur noise edge are removed if their agent loadings are diffuse, that is, if at least a third of the agents load on them beyond noise. A colluding subnetwork loads only on its own members and is not removed.

\subsection{Repeated co-movement}
\label{sec:stat}

\paragraph{Pair scores.} At each time step $t$, we let $\cos_{ij}^{(t)}$ be the cosine between the increments of agents $i$ and $j$ for every pair observed at that time step. We replace each cosine by its rank among the $n_t$ observed pairs and transform the rank to a normal score,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pairscore}
q_{ij}^{(t)} \;=\; \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\cos_{ij}^{(t)}) - \tfrac12}{n_t}\Big).
\end{equation}
Ranking within each time step makes every time step count equally, whatever the size of the platform's movement at that time step, and makes the pair scores insensitive to the scale and distribution of the increments.

\paragraph{Set scores.} The score of a candidate set $S$ at time step $t$ is the standardized sum of its observed pair scores,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:setscore}
Z_S^{(t)} \;=\; \binom{|S|}{2}^{-1/2}\sum_{i<j\in S} q_{ij}^{(t)},
\end{equation}
which is approximately standard normal under the null. Collusion is co-movement that repeats, so across time steps we use a ``majority statistic''. We let $L_S$ be the number of time steps at which $S$ has at least two observed members, $m_S=\lfloor L_S/2\rfloor+1$, and $M_S$ the $m_S$-th largest of $Z_S^{(1)},\dots,Z_S^{(L)}$. That is, $M_S$ is the level that $S$ exceeds at a majority of its time steps. Under independent standard normal scores, the tail probability of $M_S$ is the binomial tail of an order statistic, which we express as a $z$-score,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:majority}
z(S) \;=\; \Phi^{-1}\Big(1 - \Prob\big[\mathrm{Bin}\big(L_S, 1-\Phi(M_S)\big)\ge m_S\big]\Big),
\end{equation}
that is comparable across sets with different numbers of observed time steps. Importantly, a single time step, however extreme, cannot make $z(S)$ large, while a set that moves together moderately at most time steps can.

\subsection{Scan and permutation null}
\label{sec:scan}

\paragraph{Candidate family.} The family $\Sfam$ of candidate sets is constructed from the data. It contains (i) every pair of agents; (ii) for every agent, the agent and its nearest neighbors by the pair statistic $z(\{i,j\})$, at sizes $5$, $10$, $20$, $40$ and $80$ (up to $N/4$); (iii) local-search refinements of the best of these sets at each size, each replaced by the agents with the largest summed pair statistic to it until it stops changing; and (iv) $k$-means clusters of the unit-normalized increment series of the agents. We divide the family into size bands $\{2\}$, $[3,10]$, $[11,30]$, $[31,100]$ and $[101,N]$. The scan statistic of band $b$ is $\Lambda_b = \max_{S\in\Sfam,\,|S|\in b} z(S)$.

\paragraph{Null distribution.} Under the null, the increments of different agents are independent given the common components. Relabeling the agents at one time step therefore leaves the joint distribution of the increments unchanged, as long as each time step is relabeled on its own. A draw from the null permutes the agent labels independently at every time step, among the agents observed at that time step, and recomputes everything that depends on the labels: the pair scores, the candidate family, and the band maxima $\Lambda_b^{(1)},\dots,\Lambda_b^{(B)}$. Because the candidate family is rebuilt inside every draw, the null accounts for the search over sets. The $p$-value of band $b$ is $p_b = (1+\#\{\beta:\Lambda_b^{(\beta)}\ge\Lambda_b\})/(B+1)$.

\paragraph{Global $p$-value.} The bands search overlapping sets, so their $p$-values are strongly dependent and a Bonferroni correction across bands is conservative. We instead calibrate the smallest band $p$-value by its own permutation distribution \citep{westfall1993resampling}. The band $p$-values of every draw are computed against the other $B$ draws and the observed data -- all $B+1$ of which are exchangeable under the null -- and the global $p$-value is the share of the $B+1$ minima that are at most the observed minimum. The test is exact under the null.

\subsection{Membership}
\label{sec:membership}

When the null is rejected, we let $S_0$ be the best set of the most significant band. We estimate membership by scoring every agent against $S_0$ and separating the members from the other agents with a two-groups model \citep{efron2004large}.

\paragraph{Agent scores.} At each time step, the mean pair score of agent $i$ with the observed members of $S_0$ (other than $i$) is ranked among all observed agents and transformed to a normal score $v_i^{(t)}$. The score of agent $i$ is $s_i = L_i^{-1/2}\sum_t v_i^{(t)}$ over its $L_i$ observed time steps. Ranking among agents calibrates the score regardless of the correlation among the members of $S_0$. Summing uses every time step, and since each normal score is bounded by $\Phi^{-1}(1-1/2N)$, no single time step can make an agent a member.

\paragraph{Two-groups model.} We model the scores as $s_i \sim (1-\pi)\,N(\mu_0,\tau^2) + \pi\,N(\mu_1,\tau^2)$ with $\mu_1>\mu_0$, fitted by EM. The null component is estimated rather than fixed at $N(0,1)$ -- an ``empirical null'' -- because the members take the top ranks at every time step, which shifts the scores of the other agents down. The two components share a variance, so that the posterior probability of membership increases with the score. The estimate $\hat S$ is the set of agents whose posterior probability of membership $w_i$ exceeds $1/2$, the Bayes rule for misclassification, and it is recomputed against the new set until it stops changing. Members are reported with their scores $s_i$, which grade the strength of each member's attachment.

\subsection{Several subnetworks}
\label{sec:peel}

We find several subnetworks by ``peeling'': we test, estimate $\hat S$, set its members aside, and test the remaining agents again, stopping at the first round whose $p$-value exceeds $\alpha$. Each round is a level-$\alpha$ test of the null among the agents not yet set aside. A round whose estimate has a single member does not yield a subnetwork; that agent is set aside and peeling continues.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Detecting \& estimating colluding subnetworks}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE {\bfseries Input:} $\D^{(1)},\dots,\D^{(T)}$, $\Gamma$, $B$, $\alpha$
\STATE $\hat\psi_i^{(t)}\leftarrow$ kernel PCA of $\Gamma$ over all units \hfill(Sec.~\ref{sec:est-ps})
\STATE $\hat\delta_i^{(t)}\leftarrow$ split-half increments \hfill\eqref{eq:increment}
\STATE $\mathcal A\leftarrow\N$
\LOOP
  \STATE $q_{ij}^{(t)}$, $i,j\in\mathcal A$ \eqref{eq:pairscore}; \ $\Lambda_b\leftarrow\max_{S\in\Sfam,\,|S|\in b} z(S)$ \eqref{eq:majority}
  \FOR{$\beta=1$ {\bfseries to} $B$}
    \STATE permute labels at each $t$; recompute $\Lambda_b^{(\beta)}$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $p\leftarrow$ permutation min-$p$ over bands \hfill(Sec.~\ref{sec:scan})
  \STATE {\bfseries if} $p>\alpha$ {\bfseries then stop}
  \STATE $\hat S\leftarrow$ best set of the most significant band
  \REPEAT
    \STATE $s_i\leftarrow$ score of $i$ against $\hat S$ \hfill(Sec.~\ref{sec:membership})
    \STATE $w_i\leftarrow$ posterior of the two-groups model fit to $\{s_i\}$
    \STATE $\hat S\leftarrow\{i\in\mathcal A: w_i>1/2\}$
  \UNTIL{$\hat S$ is unchanged}
  \STATE {\bfseries if} $|\hat S|\ge2$ {\bfseries then} report $(\hat S,p)$; \ $\mathcal A\leftarrow\mathcal A\setminus\hat S$
\ENDLOOP
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Why the construction works}
\label{sec:theory}

The guarantee that motivates the construction is the concentration of perspective space representations around their population counterparts. Under the assumptions of \citet[Theorem~1]{pkps2026} -- bounded kernels and embeddings, positive cross-unit query-kernel mass $\kappa = \inf_{a,a'}\E[k_\Q(u,u')]$, and a rank-$d$ spectral gap $\lambda_d$ in the population Gram matrix -- if every unit has at least $m$ responses, then with probability at least $1-\eta$ there is an orthogonal $W$ such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conc}
\max_a \|\hat\psi_a - W\psi_a\| \;\le\; \varepsilon_m
\;:=\; C\,\kappa^{-2}\lambda_d^{-1/2}\sqrt{\frac{d\,n\log(4n^2/\eta)}{2m}} ,
\end{equation}
where $C$ depends only on the kernel bounds and $n=NT$ is the number of units. Consequently, every increment is estimated to within $2\varepsilon_m$, at the rate of the paired setting: the method does not require any two agents to have responded to a common query and pays for unpairedness only through $\kappa^{-2}$. Notably, the test does not rely on Eq.~\eqref{eq:conc} for its validity -- the per-step permutation is exact under the null for any estimation error. Eq.~\eqref{eq:conc} instead governs power, through the share of each increment that is movement rather than estimation error. \todo{Check that the bound of \citet{pkps2026} covers the conditioning of Section~\ref{sec:est-ps}, and state and prove the consistency of the test and of the two-groups estimate of membership as $m$ and $T$ grow.}

\section{Simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

We next characterize the method in a synthetic multi-agent system in the vantablack setting, under controlled conditions where the colluding subnetworks are known. We evaluate detection (P0), membership estimation (P1), and the recovery of several subnetworks (P2), and we compare the method against an oracle that knows where to look.

\subsection{Set up}
\label{sec:sim-model}

\paragraph{Perspectives.} Agent $i$ has a latent perspective $z_i^{(t)}\in\R^{10}$ at time $t$, with $z_i^{(1)}=\eta_i\sim N(0,I_{10})$ and $z_i^{(t+1)} = z_i^{(t)}+\delta_i^{(t)}$. The increments follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} with $c^{(t)}=0$ and $\sigma_i^{(t)}=1$. That is, every agent moves the same amount at every time step, and a share $\rho$ of the movement of each member of a colluding subnetwork is shared with the other members. Members of different subnetworks follow independent group processes.

\paragraph{Queries and responses.} The query space has $1{,}000$ sub-topic centers $c_j\sim N(0,I_{50})$. Each response answers its own query $u=c_j+0.9\,\xi$, with $j$ drawn uniformly and $\xi\sim N(0,I_{50})$, so that no two responses share a query. The observed query is $\hat u = u + 0.5\,\zeta$ with $\zeta\sim N(0,I_{50})$ -- the vantablack setting. The embedded response of agent $i$ at time $t$ to query $u$ is
\begin{equation}
x \;=\; M(\tilde u)\, z_i^{(t)} + W\tilde u + \varepsilon,\qquad M(\tilde u) = M_0 + \textstyle\sum_k \tilde u_k M_k,\quad \varepsilon\sim N(0,I_{100}),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde u = u/\sqrt{1+0.9^2}$, $M_0\in\R^{100\times 10}$ has orthonormal columns, and the $M_k$ and $W$ are fixed Gaussian matrices. The query therefore changes both what every agent says ($W\tilde u$) and how an agent's perspective is expressed ($M(\tilde u)$). Each agent gives $m$ responses per time step.

\paragraph{Design.} Unless otherwise stated, $N=500$, $m=20$, $T=8$, and there is one colluding subnetwork with $|S|=20$. Starting from this base configuration, we vary one factor at a time: the number of agents $N\in\{100,200,500,1000\}$, the number of responses per agent per time step $m\in\{5,10,20,50,100\}$, the size of the subnetwork $|S|\in\{2,5,10,20,50,100\}$, and the number of time steps $T\in\{3,4,8,16,32\}$. Each cell is crossed with $\rho\in\{0.2,0.3,0.5\}$ and, for $N$, $m$ and $T$, with $\rho=0$. For several subnetworks, $60$ colluders are split into $K\in\{2,3,6,10\}$ subnetworks of $60/K$ agents each. $T=2$ is excluded: it yields a single increment, with which co-movement cannot repeat. Each cell has $50$ replicates.

\paragraph{Method and oracle.} The method is run as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:main} with the kernels of Section~\ref{sec:est-ps} and $B=99$ permutations, with no tuning to the simulation. Peeling is used only when there are several subnetworks. The oracle knows $S$. Its test applies the statistic $z(S)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:majority} to the true set, calibrated by the same per-step permutation, and its membership estimate is a single two-groups pass against the true set. The gap between the method and the oracle is therefore the price of not knowing where to look.

\paragraph{Error criteria.} Type~I error is the rejection rate at $\alpha=0.05$ when $\rho=0$, and power is the rejection rate when $\rho>0$. Membership is evaluated by the precision $|\hat S\cap S|/|\hat S|$ and recall $|\hat S\cap S|/|S|$ of $\hat S$, computed in every replicate whether or not the test rejects, so that a detector with no power cannot look good on membership. With several subnetworks, a subnetwork is ``recovered'' if a reported subnetwork has Jaccard index at least $0.5$ with it.

\paragraph{The test controls Type~I error.} Over the $600$ null replicates, the test rejected $29$ times at $\alpha=0.05$, a rate of $0.048$ ($95\%$ confidence interval $0.033$--$0.069$), and the rate in every null cell is between $0.02$ and $0.08$. Figure~\ref{fig:sim-detect}(a) shows that the null distribution of the $p$-value is uniform. That is, the per-step permutation calibrates the scan, including the search over candidate sets.

\paragraph{Power is governed by repetition and by the share of the system that colludes.} In the base configuration, power is $0.14$, $0.82$, and $1.00$ at $\rho=0.2$, $0.3$, and $0.5$. Figure~\ref{fig:sim-detect}(b)--(e) shows that power increases with the number of responses per agent, the size of the subnetwork, and the number of time steps, and decreases with the number of agents when the subnetwork has a fixed size. At $\rho=0.3$, for example, power is $0.98$ with $N=100$ agents and $0.58$ with $N=1{,}000$, and it rises from $0.04$ with $T=3$ time steps to $1.00$ with $T=16$. Notably, the number of time steps acts as an effect size: a weak but persistent subnetwork ($\rho=0.2$) is detected in $86\%$ of replicates with $T=16$ and in every replicate with $T=32$.

\paragraph{The price of searching is largest for small subnetworks and short horizons.} The oracle rejects in nearly every cell with $\rho>0$. The gap between the method and the oracle is concentrated where the subnetwork is small or the horizon is short. With $|S|=5$ and $\rho=0.5$, for example, the method rejects in $10\%$ of replicates and the oracle in all of them. Since the oracle uses the same statistic and the same null, the gap reflects the difficulty of finding a small set among $\binom{N}{|S|}$ candidates, not a weakness of the statistic.

\paragraph{Membership is recovered when the subnetwork is detected.} Figure~\ref{fig:sim-member} shows that the precision and recall of $\hat S$ track power. In the base configuration with $\rho=0.3$, precision and recall are $0.79$ and $0.76$, against $0.87$ and $0.81$ for the oracle; with $\rho=0.5$, both the method and the oracle reach $0.96$ and $0.98$. The largest gaps again occur where detection is hard: with $N=1{,}000$ and $\rho=0.3$, precision and recall are $0.61$ and $0.60$, against $0.82$ and $0.81$ for the oracle.

\paragraph{Peeling recovers several subnetworks until they become small.} Figure~\ref{fig:sim-detect}(f) shows that, with $\rho=0.5$, peeling recovers $100\%$, $100\%$, $88\%$, and $30\%$ of the subnetworks when $60$ colluders are split into $K=2$, $3$, $6$, and $10$ subnetworks. The oracle test rejects for at least $99\%$ of the subnetworks whenever $\rho\ge 0.3$. Recovery therefore breaks down as the subnetworks shrink to $6$--$10$ members -- consistent with the loss of power for small subnetworks in Figure~\ref{fig:sim-detect}(d).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We introduced a definition of colluding subnetworks -- subsets of agents whose behavior repeatedly moves together -- and a method for detecting and estimating them from black-box behavior alone. The method jointly embeds every (agent, time) pair in a shared perspective space, scans for repeated co-movement, and calibrates the scan by permuting agent labels at every time step. In simulation, the test controlled Type~I error, and its power and membership recovery approached those of an oracle that knows the subnetwork when co-movement was strong and repeated over enough time steps. On Moltbook, the method detected eight colluding subnetworks, five of which are corroborated by shared account names that the method never sees. Four of the eight were recovered under nearly every change of embedding function, dimension, horizon and amount of data; the other four were near the limit of detection.

\paragraph{From colluding to adversarial.}
Definition~\ref{def:collusion} is behavioral: it flags a subset of agents that repeatedly moved together. Whether the co-movement is harmful is a separate question that requires a score. When a score $\sigma^{(t)}(A)$ on subsets of agents is available, a natural definition of an \emph{adversarial} subnetwork is one for which the score of the agents outside it, $\sigma^{(t)}(\N\setminus S)$, is lower than it would have been had the members of $S$ not moved together. The difference is a treatment effect of $S$ on the other agents, and given $\hat S$ it can be estimated by extrapolating trends from before the co-movement or by synthetic control from agents outside $\hat S$. We kept scores out of the detection problem deliberately: on the platforms that motivate this work, the score is observed only sometimes and is defined by the platform rather than by the observer, while the behavioral signal is always observed. A detected subnetwork is the input to that assessment and to any mitigation that follows. The interventions available to a platform operator -- steering the environment or the score, quarantining edges, removing agents, resetting state -- act on components that the observer cannot touch. In the vantablack setting, the output of our method is therefore a recommendation rather than an action.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several aspects of our approach are simplified relative to real deployments. (i) \emph{Repetition.} Collusion is identified by repetition. A subnetwork that coordinates once, or only at a minority of time steps, is not what the majority statistic is built to find, and with few time steps or small subnetworks the scan loses much of the power of a test that knows the subnetwork -- the price of searching. (ii) \emph{Shared stimuli.} The definition does not separate coordination from a shared stimulus that only the members respond to (Section~\ref{sec:collusion}), and shared structure is removed only when it is diffuse. A niche of agents that follows its own topic over time is therefore reported as a subnetwork. (iii) \emph{Exchangeability.} The per-step permutation is exact when the increments of different agents are exchangeable at each time step after the common components are removed. Ranking within time steps handles agents that differ persistently in how much they move, but not every form of heterogeneity. (iv) \emph{The candidate family.} The family is data-driven, and a subnetwork whose members are not near one another in the pair statistic can be missed even when its aggregate co-movement is strong. (v) \emph{Disjoint subnetworks.} Several subnetworks are found by sequential testing and are assumed to be disjoint. (vi) \emph{Fixed representations.} The kernel and the embedding functions $g_\Q$ and $g_\X$ are fixed in advance. A colluding subnetwork whose shared change is invisible to the kernel is invisible to the method -- a limitation shared by every response-based representation.

\paragraph{Future work.}
On Moltbook, we reported the subnetworks found by the method alone. Whether the members of a subnetwork share an operator, a script, or only a common configuration is a separate question, which other channels of the same data could inform: the text of the posts (e.g., shared templates), posting times, and replies between members. Combining these channels with repeated co-movement -- both to corroborate a detection and to characterize it -- is a natural next step. On the theoretical side, establishing that the pair and set scores of a colluding subnetwork separate from those of other sets as the number of responses and time steps grows, and that the two-groups estimate of membership is consistent, would complete the guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. More broadly, the method requires nothing from the agents beyond their observed behavior and nothing from the platform beyond a log of who did what in which context. We view the detection of colluding subnetworks as one component of a monitoring stack for open multi-agent systems -- alongside agent- and group-level change detection and system-level control charts -- with the perspective space as the shared object that lets these components work together.
