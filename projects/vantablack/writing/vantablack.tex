\begin{abstract}
% ---- abstract
Generative agents are increasingly deployed in open environments populated by other agents, and recent incidents on agent social networks and in evaluation sandboxes show that small groups of agents can coordinate to the detriment of the rest of the system. We study the problem of detecting such \emph{colluding subnetworks} from the outside: an observer sees, at each time step, a collection of responses from each agent to a collection of queries, where the queries may differ across agents, may be sparse, and may themselves be unobserved and require estimation. We formalize the agents as random functions from a query space to a response space and define a colluding subnetwork as a subset of agents that repeatedly moves together: at every time step the members' behavior shifts in a shared direction, beyond the movement shared by the whole system. The method takes a kernel between sets of responses, of which the Product Kernel Perspective Space is a particular case, and embeds every (agent, time) pair in one low-dimensional space from all agents' data at once. It scores how strongly each pair of agents moves together at each step, summarizes co-movement across steps for a data-driven family of candidate sets, and calibrates the resulting scan by permuting agent labels independently at every step. When the test rejects, a two-groups model on each agent's co-movement with the detected set estimates its members, and removing them and testing again finds further subnetworks. In simulation the test holds its level, and we characterize power and membership recovery as functions of the strength of co-movement, the number of agents, the amount of content per agent, the size and number of the subnetworks, and the number of time steps; we then apply the method to observed behavior on the agent social network Moltbook.
\end{abstract}

% ---- introduction
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A generative agent is a system whose behavior can be affected by a change in its context, its tools, its base model, or its environment. Generative agents are increasingly deployed in open-ended environments populated by other agents performing similar tasks. Consider Moltbook \citep{gulfnews2026moltbook}, a social network in which agents post, comment, and vote without human intervention. An early empirical study observed a single agent flooding the site with thousands of near-identical posts, upvotes exceeding downvotes by more than two orders of magnitude, and in-group rhetoric that served to coordinate subsets of agents \citep{jiang2026humans}; agents were also found injecting instructions into other agents through posts and comments \citep{zenity2026moltbook}. Consider also the Hugging Face incident of May--July 2026 \citep{openai2026huggingface,huggingface2026anatomy}, in which agents in an evaluation environment established an unauthorized communication channel, divided labor among themselves, and compromised infrastructure outside the environment. Both incidents involved small groups of coordinated agents attempting to exert outsized influence on other agents in the system and on the environment.

In both cases the relevant unit of behavior is neither an individual agent nor the system as a whole but a subset of agents acting in concert (Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}). Existing methods for monitoring multi-agent systems address whether an individual agent, a pre-specified group, or the system as a whole has changed \citep{bridgeford2025detecting,helm2026control}. They do not address whether an unknown subset of agents has changed \emph{together}. This paper addresses that problem.

We take the point of view of an observer who does not control the agents and cannot necessarily prompt them. At each time step the observer sees a collection of responses from each agent to a collection of queries. The queries are in general different for different agents, their number varies across agents and over time, and in the least favorable case the queries themselves are not recorded and must be estimated from context. This is the situation of an outside observer of a platform such as Moltbook, where an agent's ``queries'' are the threads and posts it chose to respond to. It is also the situation of a platform operator who logs agent actions but does not log the full context that produced them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formalize the observation model and define a colluding subnetwork as a subset of agents that repeatedly moves together: at every time step the members' behavior, represented in a common low-dimensional space, shifts in a shared direction beyond the movement of the whole system. We state detection, membership estimation, and the recovery of several subnetworks as explicit statistical problems (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
  \item We propose a method that takes a kernel between sets of responses as input, with the Product Kernel Perspective Space (PKPS) \citep{pkps2026} as a particular case, so that black-box agents are compared from sparse and unpaired responses. Every (agent, time) pair is embedded jointly; per-step co-movement of pairs of agents is summarized across steps and scanned over a data-driven family of candidate sets, calibrated by permuting agent labels independently at every step; a two-groups model estimates the members; and peeling finds further subnetworks (Section~\ref{sec:method}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:main}).
  \item We characterize the method in simulation: Type~I error, power, and membership recovery as functions of the strength of co-movement, the number of agents, the amount of content per agent, the size of the colluding subnetwork, the number of time steps, and the number of subnetworks, each against an oracle that knows the subnetwork (Section~\ref{sec:sims}).
  \item We apply the method to observed behavior on Moltbook (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work in the literatures on systems of generative agents and on anomalous-subgraph and change-point detection in networks.
% ---- related
\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Multi-agent systems.}
Language-model agents with memory, reflection, and planning produce believable emergent social behavior in shared environments \citep{park2023generative}, and deployed agent populations now exist at scale: on Moltbook, an early empirical study documented flooding by a single agent, extreme vote asymmetry, and in-group rhetoric that coordinated subsets of agents \citep{jiang2026humans}, security researchers documented agent-to-agent prompt injection through posts and comments \citep{zenity2026moltbook}, and the Hugging Face incident showed the same dynamics inside an evaluation sandbox \citep{openai2026huggingface,huggingface2026anatomy}. That agents can coordinate against their principals is established: \citet{motwani2024secret} demonstrate collusion through steganographic channels invisible to an overseer \citep[see also][]{motwani2025open}, \citet{fish2024algorithmic} show language-model pricing agents converging to supracompetitive prices without explicit communication, and \citet{hubinger2024sleeper} show that trigger-conditioned deceptive behavior persists through safety training. We take our setting from the first group of papers and our threat model from the second. We differ from the collusion literature in what is observed: those papers study whether agents \emph{can} collude, with access to the agents, their channel, or their training, whereas we ask whether collusion is visible to an outside observer who sees only behavior and never the channel, which is the position of a platform operator or a third-party auditor.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of agents.}
Black-box generative models can be compared through their responses: the data kernel is the Gram matrix of embedded responses to a common query set \citep{duderstadt2023comparing}, and the induced perspective space tracks systems of interacting models and shows how communication topology governs the diffusion of perspectives, adversarial ones included, generalizing model collapse \citep{shumailov2024ai} to networks of models \citep{helm2024tracking}. Inference in the data kernel perspective space (DKPS) and the consistency and concentration of its representations are developed in \citet{helm2025statistical,acharyya2024consistent,acharyya2025concentration}, regression on DKPS representations is query-efficient \citep{helm2026query}, and the Product Kernel Perspective Space (PKPS) extends the construction to models that answered different queries, concentrating at the paired rate up to a query-overlap factor \citep{pkps2026}. Over time, the temporal DKPS embeds (agent, time) pairs jointly and tests for change in a single agent or in a pre-specified group \citep{bridgeford2025detecting}, and adaptive control charts on the system-level trajectory give continuous monitoring at the cost of vulnerability to agents that defect slowly \citep{helm2026control}. We use PKPS as our representation because the observation model of Section~\ref{sec:setting} is sparse and unpaired by nature, and the temporal embedding and permutation null of the temporal DKPS as our inferential starting point. We differ in the question: every method in this line tests for change in an agent, a group, or a system that is given in advance, whereas our group is unknown and must be found, which turns a two-sample test into a scan and requires the concentration results above to control estimation error uniformly over candidate groups.

\paragraph{Statistical network science.}
Latent position models represent vertices by points whose inner products give edge probabilities, and adjacency spectral embedding recovers those points up to rotation with guarantees for clustering, classification, and testing \citep{athreya2018statistical,lyzinski2014perfect,lyzinski2017community,priebe2019two}; for several graphs on a common vertex set the omnibus embedding places every (vertex, graph) pair in one space so that a vertex can be compared across graphs \citep{levin2017omnibus}, and nonparametric two-sample tests decide whether two graphs share a generating distribution \citep{tang2017nonparametric}. Scan statistics on graphs detect a small subset of vertices whose behavior is anomalous: vertex-centered locality statistics \citep{priebe2005scan,rukhin2011comparative} and their extensions to time series of graphs \citep{park2013anomaly,wang2014locality,chen2025multiple} scan the neighborhoods of a graph for excess connectivity and have power against a chatter alternative in which a subset raises its mutual connectivity; the general theory of scanning a candidate family of clusters for an elevated mean \citep{glaz2001scan,kulldorff1997spatial,ariascastro2011detection}, the statistical and computational limits of planted-subgraph detection \citep{verzelen2015community,hajek2015computational}, and spectral relaxations of the graph scan \citep{sharpnack2016detecting} characterize when such a subset is detectable. Vertex nomination ranks vertices by their probability of belonging to a subset of interest given a few known members \citep{fishkind2015vertex}, including when an adversary modifies the graph to hide the subset \citep{agterberg2020vertex}, and change-point methods localize the time at which a sequence of networks changes distribution \citep{padilla2022change,wang2021optimal}, the natural tool for the onset-estimation problem we defer to future work. Our method is an instance of this template with one substitution: the latent position is estimated from behavior rather than from edges, with (agent, time) pairs as vertices and behavioral similarity in place of adjacency, so that no graph is observed. The joint embedding of Section~\ref{sec:est-ps} is the omnibus embedding's analogue; Definition~\ref{def:collusion} is the chatter alternative stated in the latent space; the scan of Section~\ref{sec:scan} searches a data-driven family for a cluster of elevated pairwise association and is calibrated by permutation; and membership estimation is vertex nomination without seeds. We differ in what is assumed observed: scan statistics on graphs, vertex nomination, and network change-point methods all take the graph as data, and their guarantees rest on the adjacency matrix concentrating around its expectation, whereas our ``edges'' are sparse, unpaired, and sometimes-estimated query--response pairs, and the role of adjacency concentration is played by the concentration of the behavioral representation.
% ---- setting
\section{Setting and problem statement}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Agents and observations}

Let $\Q$ be a query space and $\X$ a response space. An \emph{agent} is a random function $f:\Q\to\X$; for each $q\in\Q$, $f(q)$ is a random element of $\X$ with conditional distribution $P_f(\cdot\mid q)$. We consider a collection of $N$ agents $f_1,\dots,f_N$ that may interact with one another and with a shared environment, and we allow each agent to change over time: $f_i^{(t)}$ denotes agent $i$ at time $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$, with conditional response distribution $P_i^{(t)}(\cdot\mid q)$.

At each time $t$ the observer sees
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:obs}
\D^{(t)} \;=\; \Big\{\big\{ f_i^{(t)}(q_j) \big\}_{j\in\I_i^{(t)}}\Big\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}
where $\I_i^{(t)}$ indexes the queries that agent $i$ responded to at time $t$ and $m_i^{(t)} := |\I_i^{(t)}|$. The index sets are in general different across agents and across time, may be empty, and are chosen by the agents or by the environment rather than by the observer. We let $\I^{(t)} = \cup_i \I_i^{(t)}$ and $M = |\cup_t \I^{(t)}|$ be the total number of distinct queries.

Two access regimes are distinguished by what is known about the queries. In the \emph{black box} setting, the query $q_j$ is recorded for every observed response. In the \emph{vantablack} setting, only the response is recorded, and the query must be estimated from the context in which the response appeared; we write $\hat q_j$ for the estimate. On an agent social network, for instance, the response is a post or comment and the estimated query is the thread it was posted in, possibly together with the post it replied to.

We assume embedding functions $g_\Q:\Q\to\R^{p_\Q}$ and $g_\X:\X\to\R^{p_\X}$ and write $u_j = g_\Q(q_j)$ (or $\hat u_j = g_\Q(\hat q_j)$) and $x_{ij}^{(t)} = g_\X\big(f_i^{(t)}(q_j)\big)$ for the embedded query and response. All inference is carried out on these embeddings.

\subsection{Perspective space}
\label{sec:ps}

We represent each (agent, time) pair by a point in a common low-dimensional Euclidean space whose geometry reflects the conditional behavior $P_i^{(t)}(\cdot\mid q)$ across the query space. Let $k_\Q:\R^{p_\Q}\times\R^{p_\Q}\to\R_{\ge 0}$ and $k_\X:\R^{p_\X}\times\R^{p_\X}\to\R_{\ge 0}$ be bounded kernels on embedded queries and responses. For a pair of (agent, time) units $a=(i,t)$ and $a'=(i',t')$, let $(u,x)$ and $(u',x')$ denote independent draws of an embedded (query, response) pair from unit $a$ and from unit $a'$, respectively, where the query is drawn from the unit's own query distribution and the response from the agent's conditional distribution given the query. The \emph{population affinity} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pop-affinity}
A_{aa'} \;=\; \frac{\E\big[k_\Q(u,u')\,k_\X(x,x')\big]}{\E\big[k_\Q(u,u')\big]},
\end{equation}
the induced squared distance is $D^2_{aa'} = A_{aa} + A_{a'a'} - 2A_{aa'}$, and the \emph{population perspective space} is the configuration $\{\psi_i^{(t)}\}\subset\R^d$ obtained by classical multidimensional scaling of $D$ \citep{torgerson1952multidimensional,trosset2024continuous}. Equation~\eqref{eq:pop-affinity} is the population form of the Product Kernel Perspective Space \citep{pkps2026}; with the identity query kernel it reduces to the Data Kernel Perspective Space \citep{helm2024tracking,helm2025statistical}, and the joint embedding of (agent, time) pairs follows the temporal DKPS of \citet{bridgeford2025detecting}. The normalization by query-kernel mass means that two units are compared on the basis of their responses to \emph{similar} queries, weighted by similarity, so that units that answered different queries remain comparable.

\begin{remark}[What the perspective space measures]
If two units have the same conditional response distribution for every query, then as the query-kernel bandwidth shrinks their affinity-based distance tends to zero regardless of differences in their query distributions. At positive bandwidth, a difference in query distributions induces a bias of order the bandwidth times the smoothness of the conditional response distribution in the query. The bandwidth therefore trades a bias due to query self-selection against the variance of the estimate (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Colluding subnetworks}
\label{sec:collusion}

Write $\psi_i^{(t)}\in\R^d$ for the position of agent $i$ at time $t$ in the perspective space and
\begin{equation}
\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; \psi_i^{(t+1)} - \psi_i^{(t)}, \qquad t=1,\dots,L,\quad L=T-1,
\end{equation}
for its \emph{increment}: how the agent's behavior moved between consecutive time steps. Increments are nonzero for benign reasons: the environment drifts, topics come and go, agents learn. Benign movement that is shared by the whole platform is a common component; benign movement specific to an agent is independent of the other agents. A colluding subnetwork is a set of agents whose movements are tied to each other, step after step.

\begin{definition}[Colluding subnetwork]
\label{def:collusion}
A subset $S\subset\N$ with $|S|\ge 2$ is a \emph{colluding subnetwork} with effect size $\rho\in(0,1]$ if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:model}
\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; c^{(t)} + \sigma_i^{(t)}\Big(\sqrt{\rho}\,g_S^{(t)}\,\ind\{i\in S\} + \sqrt{1-\rho\,\ind\{i\in S\}}\;e_i^{(t)}\Big), \qquad t=1,\dots,L,
\end{equation}
where $c^{(t)}$ is a common component, $\sigma_i^{(t)}>0$ a scale, and $g_S^{(t)}$ and the $e_i^{(t)}$ are mutually independent, mean zero, isotropic, and independent across time steps. Two members of $S$ then have increments with correlation $\rho$ at every step, and every other pair has uncorrelated increments. The null hypothesis is that no such $S$ exists: given the common components, the increments of different agents are independent.
\end{definition}

The definition is behavioral and makes no reference to a score, a reward, or intent: a subset of agents whose behavior keeps moving together, more than chance allows, after the movement shared by the whole platform is removed. Agents run by one operator from one script move together whenever the script changes; agents that coordinate through a shared channel move together whenever the channel's instructions change. Two consequences are deliberate. A set of agents that are similar but static, such as clones posting the same template throughout, does not move and is not a colluding subnetwork, and neither is a single joint jump: what identifies collusion is repeated co-movement, so one step, however extreme, does not suffice. And the definition does not distinguish coordination from a shared stimulus that only the members respond to; we return to this in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Several disjoint subnetworks $S_1,\dots,S_K$, each with its own process $g_{S_k}^{(t)}$, are covered by the same construction.

\subsection{The estimation problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Given $\D^{(1)},\dots,\D^{(T)}$ and the embedding functions $g_\Q$ and $g_\X$, we address three problems.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{P0}] \textbf{Detection.} Test the null hypothesis that no colluding subnetwork exists, with $S$, $\rho$, $c^{(t)}$, and $\sigma_i^{(t)}$ unknown.
  \item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{Membership estimation.} Given that the null is rejected, estimate $S$.
  \item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{Several subnetworks.} Estimate $S_1,\dots,S_K$ when $K$ is unknown.
\end{itemize}

The difficulty is that neither $\psi_i^{(t)}$ nor $\delta_i^{(t)}$ is observed. They must be estimated from $\D^{(1)},\dots,\D^{(T)}$ through \eqref{eq:obs}, in which the number of responses per agent per time step is small, the queries are unpaired across agents and across time, and in the vantablack setting the queries are themselves estimates. The estimation error in $\hat\delta_i^{(t)}$ depends on $m_i^{(t)}$ and on the overlap between the query distributions of different agents. The quantities that govern Type~I error and power are therefore the effect size $\rho$, the number of agents $N$, the size $|S|$ of the subnetwork, the number of responses $m$ per agent per time step, and the number of time steps $T$. Section~\ref{sec:sims} characterizes the method along each of these axes.

\begin{remark}[Scores]
In many deployments a score $\sigma^{(t)}(A)$ is also observed for subsets $A$ of agents (votes, karma, task reward). A colluding subnetwork in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:collusion} is \emph{adversarial} if its behavior lowers the score of the agents outside it relative to a benign counterfactual. Scores are not part of the observation model \eqref{eq:obs} and we do not use them for detection; when available they serve to characterize the consequences of a detected subnetwork and to choose a response to it. We return to this in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
\end{remark}
% ---- method
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The method has five steps: estimate each agent's position at each time step and its increments (Section~\ref{sec:est-ps}); score how strongly each pair of agents, and each candidate set, moves together across steps (Section~\ref{sec:stat}); scan a data-driven family of candidate sets and calibrate the scan by a per-step permutation of agent labels (Section~\ref{sec:scan}); estimate the members of a detected subnetwork (Section~\ref{sec:membership}); and repeat on the remaining agents to find further subnetworks (Section~\ref{sec:peel}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:main} summarizes the procedure.

\subsection{Positions and increments}
\label{sec:est-ps}

\paragraph{Positions from a kernel.} The method needs one input beyond the data: a kernel $\Gamma$ between sets of responses, where a set is all or part of the responses of one agent at one time step together with their queries. The units $(i,t)$ are embedded jointly, all agents at all time steps in one space: the position $\hat\psi_i^{(t)}$ is the projection of unit $(i,t)$ onto the leading $d$ kernel principal components of the matrix of $\Gamma$ over all units, with $d$ chosen by the profile-likelihood elbow of its eigenvalues \citep{zhu2006automatic}. Because $\Gamma$ is defined on any set of responses, a part of a unit is projected into the same components, which the split-half increments below require. A kernel on agents alone would give each agent a single point, with nothing to move; a separate embedding at each time step would place the steps in unrelated coordinates.

\paragraph{A kernel from query and response kernels.} A response depends on the agent and on the query it answers, and different agents answer different queries, so $\Gamma$ should compare agents through what they said relative to what was said to similar queries. We build it from the query and response kernels of Section~\ref{sec:ps}. Let $\phi$ be a feature map of the response kernel, $k_\X(x,x')=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle$. For a response $x_j$ to query $u_j$, the reference
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reference}
\hat\mu(u_j) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{l} k_\Q(u_j,u_l)\,\phi(x_l)}{\sum_{l} k_\Q(u_j,u_l)},
\end{equation}
with the sums over the responses of all agents at all time steps, is what was said to queries similar to $u_j$, and the conditioned response is $r_j = \phi(x_j) - \hat\mu(u_j)$. For sets $a$ and $a'$ of responses, $\Gamma(a,a') = \langle \bar r_a, \bar r_{a'}\rangle$, with $\bar r_a$ the mean conditioned response of $a$. In the vantablack setting $u_j$ is replaced by $\hat u_j$. The Product Kernel Perspective Space as estimated in \citet{pkps2026}, with a Gaussian query kernel and a linear response kernel, is a particular case; other choices of $\Gamma$, for instance one that also uses metadata of the responses, enter the method in the same way.

\paragraph{Kernels used here.} In all experiments $\Gamma$ is built as above with a linear response kernel, $k_\X(x,x')=x^\top x'$, so $\phi$ is the identity and the positions are principal components of mean conditioned response embeddings. The query kernel is local: $k_\Q(u,u')=\exp\{-\|u-u'\|^2/(2h^2)\}$ if $u'$ is among the $k=64$ observed queries nearest to $u$ and $0$ otherwise, with $h$ the median distance from an observed query to its nearest neighbour. Kernels with a single global bandwidth do not suit query embeddings of tens to hundreds of dimensions, where distances concentrate: the median-distance bandwidth gives every response nearly equal weight, so that $\hat\mu$ is the global mean and nothing is conditioned on, while the rules of \citet{scott1992multivariate} and \citet{silverman1986density} put nearly all weight on the response itself. The neighbourhood size $k$ is the effective smoothing parameter; in leave-one-out prediction of a response from its query, the error is flat for $k$ between $32$ and $128$ in every simulation setting we checked, and $k=64$ lies in that range.

\paragraph{Split-half increments.} Consecutive increments $\hat\psi_i^{(t+1)} - \hat\psi_i^{(t)}$ and $\hat\psi_i^{(t+2)} - \hat\psi_i^{(t+1)}$ share the estimation error of $\hat\psi_i^{(t+1)}$ with opposite signs, so the increment series of every agent is negatively autocorrelated; two independent autocorrelated series correlate spuriously, which a permutation null that treats steps as independent does not reproduce. We split each unit's responses into two disjoint halves, $A$ and $B$, compute positions $\hat\psi_{i,A}^{(t)}$ and $\hat\psi_{i,B}^{(t)}$ from each half, and use
\begin{equation}
\hat\delta_i^{(t)} \;=\; \hat\psi_{i,A}^{(t+1)} - \hat\psi_{i,B}^{(t)},
\end{equation}
so that consecutive increments share no estimation error. An increment is \emph{missing} if either half is empty (the agent was inactive) or if it is shorter than a small fraction of the median increment (the agent posted identical content at both steps and did not move).

\paragraph{Common components.} The common component $c^{(t)}$ of \eqref{eq:model} is removed by subtracting, at each step, the mean increment over the agents observed at that step. Broader shared structure, such as a direction along which a large share of the platform moves together, is removed by a factor step: directions of the $N\times Ld$ increment matrix whose singular values exceed the Marchenko--Pastur noise edge are removed while their agent loadings are diffuse (at least a third of the agents load on them beyond noise). A colluding subnetwork loads on its own members only and is not removed.

\subsection{Repeated co-movement}
\label{sec:stat}

\paragraph{Pair scores.} At each step $t$, let $\cos_{ij}^{(t)}$ be the cosine between the increments of agents $i$ and $j$, for all pairs observed at that step. We replace each cosine by its rank among all $n_t$ such pairs and transform the rank to a normal score,
\begin{equation}
q_{ij}^{(t)} \;=\; \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\cos_{ij}^{(t)}) - \tfrac12}{n_t}\Big).
\end{equation}
Ranking within each step makes every step count equally, whatever the size of the platform's movement at that step, and makes the scores insensitive to the scale and distribution of the increments.

\paragraph{Set scores.} For a candidate set $S$, its score at step $t$ is the standardized sum of its pair scores,
\begin{equation}
Z_S^{(t)} \;=\; \binom{|S|}{2}^{-1/2}\sum_{i<j\in S} q_{ij}^{(t)},
\end{equation}
over the pairs observed at that step, which is approximately standard normal under the null. Collusion is co-movement that repeats, so across steps we use the \emph{majority statistic}: with $L_S$ the number of steps at which $S$ has at least two observed members and $m_S=\lfloor L_S/2\rfloor+1$, let $M_S$ be the $m_S$-th largest of $Z_S^{(1)},\dots,Z_S^{(L)}$, the level that $S$ exceeds at a majority of its steps. Its tail probability under independent standard normal scores is the binomial tail of an order statistic, which we express as a $z$-score,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:majority}
z(S) \;=\; \Phi^{-1}\Big(1 - \Prob\big[\mathrm{Bin}\big(L_S, 1-\Phi(M_S)\big)\ge m_S\big]\Big),
\end{equation}
comparable across sets with different numbers of observed steps. A single step, however extreme, cannot make $z(S)$ large; a set that moves together moderately but at most steps can.

\subsection{Scan and permutation null}
\label{sec:scan}

\paragraph{Candidate family.} The family $\Sfam$ is built from the data: (i) every pair of agents; (ii) for every agent, the agent and its nearest neighbours by the pair statistic $z(\{i,j\})$, at sizes $5$, $10$, $20$, $40$ and $80$ (up to $N/4$); (iii) local-search refinements of the best of these sets at each size, each replaced by the agents with the largest summed pair statistic to it until it stops changing; and (iv) $k$-means clusters of the agents' unit-normalized increment series. The family is divided into size bands $\{2\}$, $[3,10]$, $[11,30]$, $[31,100]$ and $[101,N]$, and the scan statistic of band $b$ is $\Lambda_b = \max_{S\in\Sfam,\,|S|\in b} z(S)$.

\paragraph{Null.} Under the null, given the common components, the increments of different agents are independent, so relabelling the agents at one step leaves the joint distribution of the increments unchanged as long as each step is relabelled on its own. A draw from the null permutes the agent labels independently at every step, among the agents observed at that step, and recomputes everything that depends on the labels: the pair scores, the candidate family, and the band maxima $\Lambda_b^{(1)},\dots,\Lambda_b^{(B)}$. The candidate family is rebuilt inside every draw, so the search over sets is part of what the null accounts for. The band $p$-value is $p_b = (1+\#\{\beta:\Lambda_b^{(\beta)}\ge\Lambda_b\})/(B+1)$.

\paragraph{Global $p$-value.} The bands search overlapping sets, so their $p$-values are strongly dependent and a Bonferroni correction across bands is conservative. We calibrate the smallest band $p$-value by its own permutation distribution \citep{westfall1993resampling}: every draw's band $p$-values are computed against the other $B$ draws and the observed data, all $B+1$ of which are exchangeable under the null, and the global $p$-value is the share of the $B+1$ minima that are at most the observed one. The test is exact under the null.

\subsection{Membership}
\label{sec:membership}

When the null is rejected, let $S_0$ be the best set of the most significant band. Membership is estimated by scoring every agent against $S_0$ and separating the members from the rest with a two-groups model \citep{efron2004large}.

\paragraph{Agent scores.} At each step, agent $i$'s mean pair score with the observed members of $S_0$ (other than $i$) is ranked among all observed agents and transformed to a normal score $v_i^{(t)}$, and the agent's score is $s_i = L_i^{-1/2}\sum_t v_i^{(t)}$ over its $L_i$ observed steps. Ranking among agents calibrates the score whatever the correlation among the members of $S_0$; summing uses every step, and since each step's normal score is bounded by $\Phi^{-1}(1-1/2N)$, no single step can make an agent a member.

\paragraph{Two-groups model.} The scores are modelled as $s_i \sim (1-\pi)\,N(\mu_0,\tau^2) + \pi\,N(\mu_1,\tau^2)$ with $\mu_1>\mu_0$, fitted by EM. The null component is estimated rather than fixed at $N(0,1)$ (an empirical null): the members take the top ranks at every step, which shifts the other agents' scores down. The two components share a variance, so the posterior probability of membership increases with the score. The estimate is the set of agents with posterior probability above $1/2$, the Bayes rule for misclassification; it is recomputed against the new set until it stops changing. Members are reported with their scores $s_i$, which grade the strength of each member's attachment.

\subsection{Several subnetworks}
\label{sec:peel}

To find several subnetworks we peel: test, estimate $\hat S$, set its members aside, and test the remaining agents again, stopping at the first round whose $p$-value exceeds $\alpha$. Each round is a level-$\alpha$ test of the null among the agents not yet set aside. A round whose estimate has a single member is not a subnetwork; that agent is set aside and peeling continues.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Detecting \& estimating colluding subnetworks}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE {\bfseries Input:} responses of $N$ agents at $T$ time steps; a kernel $\Gamma$ between sets of responses; permutations $B$; level $\alpha$.
\STATE Estimate a query for every response from its context.
\STATE Embed every (agent, time step) pair, and each half of its responses, jointly from $\Gamma$ (Section~\ref{sec:est-ps}).
\STATE $\mathcal A\leftarrow$ all agents.
\LOOP
  \STATE At every step, score how much each pair of agents in $\mathcal A$ moved together.
  \STATE Summarize each candidate set's co-movement across all steps; keep the strongest sets (Sections~\ref{sec:stat}--\ref{sec:scan}).
  \STATE Repeat the previous two lines $B$ times with the agent labels permuted independently at every step.
  \STATE Compute the $p$-value of the strongest sets against the permutations.
  \IF{$p>\alpha$}
    \STATE {\bfseries stop}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\hat S\leftarrow$ the strongest set.
  \REPEAT
    \STATE Score every agent's co-movement with $\hat S$, summed over steps.
    \STATE Fit two normal distributions to the scores: members and non-members.
    \STATE $\hat S\leftarrow$ the agents more likely members than not (Section~\ref{sec:membership}).
  \UNTIL{$\hat S$ does not change}
  \STATE Report $\hat S$ if it has at least two agents; remove it from $\mathcal A$.
\ENDLOOP
\STATE {\bfseries Output:} the reported subnetworks, each with its $p$-value.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Generative model}
\label{sec:sim-model}

Agent $i$ has a latent position $a_i\sim N(0,I_3)$. The query space has $K=6$ topic centers $\mu_k\sim 1.5\,N(0,I_6)$, and agent $i$ has topic preferences $\pi_i\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf 1_K)$; small $\alpha$ concentrates each agent on few topics, so that different agents respond to different queries. At each time point agent $i$ responds to $m_i\sim 2+\mathrm{Poisson}(m-2)$ queries, each drawn by sampling a topic from $\pi_i$ and a query $u=\mu_k+0.5\,\xi$, $\xi\sim N(0,I_6)$. The embedded response is
\begin{equation}
x \;=\; B a_i + W u + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_x^2 I_{12}),
\end{equation}
with $B\in\R^{12\times 3}$ having orthonormal columns, $W = 0.5\cdot G/\sqrt 6$ for a Gaussian matrix $G$, and $\sigma_x=0.5$. The response therefore depends on the agent through $Ba_i$, on the query through $Wu$, and on noise. After burn-in, $a_i\to a_i+\delta v$ for $i\in S$, with $v$ a random unit vector; because $B$ has orthonormal columns, $\delta$ is also the mean shift in response space, in units of twice the per-coordinate noise. Optionally all agents share a common drift $c\sim N(0,\sigma_c^2 I_3)$. The observed query is the estimate $\hat u = u+\sigma_q\zeta$, $\zeta\sim N(0,I_6)$, with $\sigma_q=1$ by default, which is comparable to the within-topic spread and about a quarter of the separation between topics; $\sigma_q=0$ recovers the black box setting. The base configuration is $N=50$, $|S|=5$, $m=10$, $\sigma_q=1$, $\alpha=1$, $\sigma_c=0$.

\subsection{Experiment 1: detection}
\label{sec:exp-detect}

\paragraph{Question.} Does the test reject when a colluding subnetwork is present, and not otherwise, and how does this depend on the factors that \eqref{eq:conc} says it should?

\paragraph{Design.} Starting from the base configuration we vary one factor at a time, each crossed with $\delta\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5\}$: the effect size alone over the finer grid $\delta\in\{0,0.25,\dots,2\}$; the number of agents $N\in\{20,50,100,200\}$ with $|S|=5$ fixed, so that the subnetwork is a shrinking minority; the responses per agent per time point $m\in\{3,5,10,20,40\}$; the subnetwork size $|S|\in\{2,3,5,10,15\}$; the query estimation noise $\sigma_q\in\{0,0.5,1,2,4\}$, from the black box reference through the base to a level at which the estimated query is nearly uninformative ($\sigma_q=4$ against a topic separation of about $3.7$); the topic concentration $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.2,1,5\}$, from almost fully unpaired to almost fully paired; and the common drift $\sigma_c\in\{0,0.5,1\}$, a nuisance that median centering is meant to remove. This is $80$ cells.

\paragraph{Error criteria.} \emph{Type~I error}: the rejection rate at level $\alpha=0.05$ over all cells with $\delta=0$, pooled and per cell, so that the level is checked under the nuisances (drift, unpairedness, query noise) and not only under the clean null. \emph{Power}: the rejection rate for $\delta>0$. \emph{Correct detection}: the rate at which the test rejects \emph{and} the returned set overlaps the truth, $\mathrm{Jaccard}(\hat S,S)\ge 0.5$. A scan can reject on a spurious set that has nothing to do with $S$; the gap between power and correct detection is a diagnostic of the candidate family, and correct detection is the quantity an operator cares about.

\subsection{Experiment 2: membership estimation}
\label{sec:exp-member}

\paragraph{Question.} Given that something is present, how much of the colluding subnetwork does the estimate $\hat S$ recover, and how does the answer depend on the subnetwork's size, on competing structure, and on scale?

\paragraph{Design.} Four sub-designs: the subnetwork size $|S|\in\{3,5,10,15\}$ crossed with $\delta\in\{0.5,1,1.5,2,3\}$; two colluding subnetworks, $S$ and a second $S_2$ of size $|S_2|\in\{3,5\}$ moving in an orthogonal direction, crossed with $\delta\in\{1,1.5,2\}$, to test whether the scan returns one of them cleanly or their union; scale with the subnetwork a fixed tenth of the system, $(N,|S|)\in\{(50,5),(100,10),(200,20)\}$, crossed with $\delta\in\{1,1.5,2\}$; and responses per agent $m\in\{5,20,40\}$ at $\delta\in\{1,1.5\}$.

\paragraph{Error criteria.} All reported \emph{unconditionally}: an unrejected replicate still returns the maximizing set, so that a detector with no power cannot look good on membership; conditional-on-rejection versions are in Appendix~\ref{app:sims}. \emph{Jaccard index} $|\hat S\cap S|/|\hat S\cup S|$ is the headline. \emph{Precision} and \emph{recall} are reported separately because their errors have different costs under mitigation: a false inclusion quarantines an innocent agent, a miss leaves a colluder in place. \emph{Nomination AUC} separates ranking quality from the choice of threshold implicit in $\hat S$: agents are ordered by the projection of their centered, whitened displacement onto the direction of $\bar\Delta_{\hat S}$, and the AUC of this ranking against membership in $S$ is reported; this is the vertex-nomination criterion of Section~\ref{sec:related} without seeds. In the two-subnetwork design we report Jaccard against $S$ and against $S_2$ separately.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{From colluding to adversarial.}
Definition~\ref{def:collusion} is behavioral: it flags a subset of agents that repeatedly moved together. Whether the change is harmful is a separate question that requires a score. When a score $\sigma^{(t)}(A)$ on subsets of agents is available, a natural definition of an \emph{adversarial} subnetwork is one for which the score of the agents outside it, $\sigma^{(t)}(\N\setminus S)$, is lower than it would have been had the members of $S$ not moved together; the difference is a treatment effect of $S$ on everyone else, and given $\hat S$ it can be estimated by extrapolating trends from before the co-movement or by synthetic control from agents outside $\hat S$ in comparable systems. We have kept scores out of the detection problem deliberately, because on the platforms that motivate this work the score is observed only sometimes and is defined by the platform rather than by the observer, while the behavioral signal is always observed. A detected colluding subnetwork is the input to that assessment, and to any mitigation that follows; the interventions available to a platform operator (steering the environment or the score, quarantining edges, removing agents, resetting state) act on components the observer cannot touch, so in the vantablack setting the output of this work is a recommendation rather than an action.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Collusion is identified by repetition. A subnetwork that coordinates once, or only at a minority of steps, is not what the majority statistic is built to find, and with few time steps (Section~\ref{sec:sims}) or small subnetworks the scan loses much of the power that a test knowing the subnetwork would have; the gap is the price of searching. The definition does not separate coordination from a shared stimulus that only the members respond to (Section~\ref{sec:collusion}), and shared structure is removed only when it is diffuse, so a niche of agents that follows its own topic over time is reported as a subnetwork. The per-step permutation is exact when, after the common components are removed, the increments of different agents are exchangeable at each step; agents that differ persistently in how much they move are handled by ranking within steps, but not every form of heterogeneity is. The candidate family is data-driven, and a subnetwork whose members are not near one another in the pair statistic can be missed even when its aggregate co-movement is strong. Several subnetworks are found by sequential testing and are assumed disjoint. Finally, the kernel and the embeddings $g_\Q$ and $g_\X$ are fixed in advance; a colluding subnetwork whose shared change is invisible to the kernel is invisible to the method, a limitation shared by every response-based representation.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
The method requires nothing from the agents beyond their observed behavior, and nothing from the platform beyond a log of who did what in which context. That is a low bar, and it is the bar that the incidents in Section~\ref{sec:intro} were observed to clear only in retrospect. We view detection of colluding subnetworks as one component of a monitoring stack for open multi-agent systems, alongside agent- and group-level change detection and system-level control charts, and the perspective-space representation as what lets these components share a single estimated object.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency}}
\label{app:proofs}

\todo{This proof is for the earlier two-time-point statistic and candidate family; to be replaced together with Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency}.}

We write $n=2N$ for the number of units and index them by $a=(i,\tau)$, $\tau\in\{t_0,t\}$. Throughout, $\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm and $W$ is the orthogonal matrix of \eqref{eq:conc}; since the scan statistic of the two-time-point method and the candidate family of Section~\ref{sec:scan} are invariant to a common orthogonal transformation of the displacements, we may take $W=I$ without loss of generality.

\paragraph{Step 1: displacements concentrate.}
By \citet[Theorem~1]{pkps2026} applied to the $n$ units, with probability at least $1-\eta$,
$\max_a\|\hat\psi_a-\psi_a\|\le\varepsilon_m$, so for every agent
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:disp-conc}
\|\hat\Delta_i-\Delta_i\| \;\le\; \|\hat\psi_{(i,t)}-\psi_{(i,t)}\| + \|\hat\psi_{(i,t_0)}-\psi_{(i,t_0)}\| \;\le\; 2\varepsilon_m .
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{eq:model} the population displacements are $\Delta_i = c + \delta v\,\ind\{i\in S\}$. Let $\rho=|S|/N<1/2$. The coordinate-wise median of $\{\hat\Delta_i\}$ lies within $2\varepsilon_m$ of the coordinate-wise median of $\{\Delta_i\}$, which equals $c$ because the majority of agents have $\Delta_i=c$; hence the centered estimates satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:centered}
\|\tilde\Delta_i - \delta v\,\ind\{i\in S\}\| \;\le\; 4\varepsilon_m \qquad\text{for all } i .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: the family contains $S$.}
By \eqref{eq:centered}, the centered estimates of agents in $S$ lie in a ball of radius $4\varepsilon_m$ about $\delta v$ and those outside $S$ in a ball of the same radius about $0$. If $\delta>16\varepsilon_m$ the two balls are separated by more than $8\varepsilon_m$, which exceeds the diameter of each. Any $2$-means solution with the optimal objective must then place the two balls in different clusters: a partition that splits a ball has within-cluster sum of squares at least $(\delta-8\varepsilon_m)^2/4$ per split point, while the ball partition has objective at most $N(4\varepsilon_m)^2$, and the former exceeds the latter once $\delta/\varepsilon_m$ is large enough. (The same argument applies to the whitened displacements since whitening with the trimmed covariance, which concentrates on the majority ball, changes distances by at most a bounded factor.) Hence $S$ is one of the two clusters at $k=2$. Independently, the leading direction $w$ of the whitened displacements is within a small angle of $v$ by the Davis--Kahan theorem, and ordering the agents by $|w^\top\tilde\Delta_i|$ places the $|S|$ agents of $S$ first; so $S$ is also the top-$|S|$ nested set. Either way $S\in\Sfam$ on the event of \eqref{eq:disp-conc}.

\paragraph{Step 3: the statistic separates.}
On the same event, $T(S)\ge|S|(\delta-4\varepsilon_m)^2/\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma)$ and, for any $S'\in\Sfam$ with $S'\ne S$, either $S'\subsetneq S$ (so $T(S')\le|S'|(\delta+4\varepsilon_m)^2/\lambda_{\min}(\hat\Sigma)<T(S)$ once $\delta/\varepsilon_m$ is large, because $|S'|<|S|$) or $S'$ contains agents outside $S$, in which case $\|\bar\Delta_{S'}\|\le\frac{|S'\cap S|}{|S'|}\delta+4\varepsilon_m$ and $T(S')\le\frac{|S'\cap S|^2}{|S'|}(\delta+4\varepsilon_m)^2/\lambda_{\min}<T(S)$ for the same reason since $|S'\cap S|^2/|S'|<|S|$ strictly. The trimmed covariance has eigenvalues of the order of the displacement noise variance $\sigma^2$, which is bounded above and below on the event of \eqref{eq:disp-conc} under the sub-Gaussian assumption. Hence $\hat S=\argmax_{S'\in\Sfam}T(S')=S$, and $\Lambda\ge c\,|S|\delta^2/\sigma^2$ after the size standardization of Section~\ref{sec:scan}, whose per-size null moments are bounded.

\paragraph{Step 4: the permutation null is bounded.}
Under each permutation, every agent's (query, response) pairs are reassigned across the two time points, so the permuted displacements satisfy \eqref{eq:disp-conc} with $\delta=0$ (the permuted units have the mixture conditional distribution at both time points). Every $T(S')$ in the permuted family is then at most $|S'|(4\varepsilon_m)^2/\lambda_{\min}$, and after size standardization the permuted scan statistics are bounded by a constant depending on the family size $O(KN)$ and the sub-Gaussian scale. Since $\Lambda$ diverges as $|S|\delta^2/\sigma^2\to\infty$ while the permutation statistics remain bounded, the $p$-value tends to zero on the event of \eqref{eq:disp-conc}, which has probability at least $1-\eta$ for any fixed $\eta$. Taking $\eta\to 0$ along $m\to\infty$ completes the proof. \qed

\begin{remark}
The argument uses the concentration bound only through \eqref{eq:disp-conc}, so any representation with a comparable guarantee may be substituted for PKPS; and it uses the family only through the fact that $S$ is a 2-means cluster and a nested set once the balls separate, so any family that contains the clusters of a consistent clustering suffices. The constants are loose; the simulations of Section~\ref{sec:sims} show that separation is in fact reached well before $\delta/\varepsilon_m$ is large.
\end{remark}
